The Hero Syndrome in Leadership: When Being Indispensable Becomes the Problem

The best leaders don't make themselves indispensable. They build teams that don't need them to be involved in everything.

There is something interesting about managers who always seem to be in the middle of every problem.

A production issue occurs, and they are the first to jump in. A project gets delayed, and they immediately take over. A stakeholder raises a concern, and they personally start coordinating the resolution. A team member struggles with a decision, and they step in to make it.

They are always available, always involved, and seemingly always busy.

From the outside, these managers often look like dedicated leaders who go above and beyond for their teams.

But I think there is a question worth asking:

Are they actually building a stronger organization, or are they building an organization that cannot function without them?

I call this the hero syndrome in leadership.

It is the tendency of managers to position themselves as the person who can solve every problem, make every important decision, and rescue every difficult situation.

Initially, this behavior might even be rewarded. Stakeholders appreciate the quick response. Senior leadership sees someone taking ownership. Teams appreciate having someone who can step in when things get difficult.

But over time, the same behavior can become one of the biggest organizational bottlenecks.

The manager who always saves the day

Consider a familiar situation in a technology organization.

A critical delivery milestone is at risk. There are dependencies across engineering, infrastructure, security, and business teams.

The manager recognizes the urgency and immediately gets involved.

They start coordinating meetings, following up with individual contributors, negotiating timelines, making technical decisions, and communicating updates to senior stakeholders.

Eventually, the milestone is delivered.

Everyone appreciates the manager's contribution.

But something important has happened along the way.

The engineers have not learned how to independently manage cross-functional dependencies. The technical leads have not developed the confidence to drive difficult conversations. The next-level managers have not had the opportunity to demonstrate ownership.

The manager has solved the immediate problem, but the organization has not necessarily become better at solving similar problems.

The next time a comparable situation occurs, everyone looks to the same manager.

And the cycle repeats.

The manager becomes busier, the team becomes increasingly dependent, and the organization starts treating individual heroics as a normal operating model.

When the same person has to repeatedly rescue the organization, perhaps the problem isn't the absence of a hero. Perhaps the problem is the operating model.

Why managers fall into the hero trap

I don't necessarily believe managers consciously set out to create dependency.

In many cases, the behavior comes from good intentions.

They want things done correctly. They don't want their teams to fail. They feel accountable for outcomes and believe that stepping in is the fastest way to get things moving.

There are also organizational incentives that reinforce this behavior.

A manager who personally resolves a critical issue is visible. A manager who quietly develops three capable leaders might receive much less recognition.

Immediate results are easier to demonstrate than long-term capability building.

But there is a difference between taking ownership and taking over.

Taking ownership means ensuring the problem gets resolved, even when the actual work is performed by someone else.

Taking over means assuming responsibility for activities and decisions that should belong to the team.

The first is leadership. The second can become dependency.

The hidden cost of being indispensable

The hero syndrome has consequences that are not always immediately visible.

1. It creates a decision-making bottleneck

When every important decision needs managerial intervention, execution slows down.

People stop evaluating alternatives independently because they expect the manager to make the final call.

Even experienced architects and engineering managers may hesitate to move forward without approval.

2. It limits the development of future leaders

People develop leadership capability by being given opportunities to lead.

If the manager always takes over difficult conversations, stakeholder escalations, and critical decisions, the next generation of leaders never gets enough exposure.

They remain good executors but struggle to become independent leaders.

3. It creates an unsustainable workload

The manager becomes the escalation point for everything.

Routine decisions, operational issues, project dependencies, and stakeholder conversations all compete for the same person's attention.

Eventually, strategic thinking gets displaced by operational firefighting.

4. It creates a false sense of organizational strength

The organization may appear to be performing well because one individual is compensating for weaknesses across the system.

But what happens when that person is unavailable, changes roles, or leaves?

The dependency becomes visible very quickly.

An organization should not confuse the effectiveness of an individual hero with the maturity of its operating model.

The difference between stepping in and taking over

I don't believe leaders should stay away from problems just to demonstrate that they are delegating.

There are situations where direct intervention is necessary.

A major security incident, a business-critical outage, a serious compliance concern, or a decision with significant enterprise-wide consequences may require immediate leadership involvement.

The distinction is in what happens after the immediate situation is under control.

For example, consider a recurring vulnerability remediation problem.

A manager could personally coordinate every application owner, chase every remediation commitment, and escalate every delay.

That might improve short-term visibility.

But an enabling leader would look at the underlying system.

Are ownership responsibilities clear? Are remediation timelines defined? Are dependencies tracked? Do application teams have the resources to upgrade? Are recurring blockers being addressed? Is there sufficient automation?

The leader should establish accountability, remove organizational barriers, and ensure the right operating mechanisms exist.

The team should own execution.

The objective is not to make the manager less accountable. It is to make the organization less dependent on the manager's personal intervention.

Your real job is to develop people who can replace you

This is perhaps the most important shift in mindset.

As managers, we should actively create opportunities for others to take ownership of responsibilities that we previously handled ourselves.

Let a technical lead run the architecture discussion.

Let an engineering manager handle a difficult stakeholder conversation.

Let a senior engineer lead a production incident review.

Let someone else present the team's progress to senior leadership.

They may not handle everything exactly the way you would.

They may make mistakes. They may need guidance. They may take longer initially.

That is part of developing capability.

The manager's role is to provide context, set expectations, establish boundaries, coach, and intervene when necessary.

It is not to ensure that everyone performs tasks in exactly the same way the manager would.

If you never give people the opportunity to operate independently, you cannot expect them to become independent.

My perspective: Stop celebrating organizational dependency

I have come to believe that being indispensable is often an overrated measure of leadership success.

A manager who personally resolves every difficult situation may be a very capable individual contributor, but that alone does not make them an effective organizational leader.

Leadership is about multiplying capability.

It is about creating systems, developing people, establishing accountability, and building an environment where good decisions can happen at every level.

I would rather see an organization where five leaders can independently manage their respective areas than one manager who personally controls all five.

I would rather see a team that can resolve problems without waiting for managerial instructions than one that consistently depends on a manager to rescue it.

And I would rather spend time developing the next generation of leaders than constantly proving that I can solve every problem myself.

There is a simple test I would encourage every manager to consider:

If your absence creates confusion, delays, and uncertainty across the organization, you may have built dependency instead of leadership capability.

Your success should not be measured by how many problems require your intervention.

It should be measured by how many problems your team can solve because of the capability you helped them develop.

Don't aim to be the hero everyone depends on.

Aim to be the leader who makes heroes unnecessary.

The strongest leaders don't build organizations around themselves. They build organizations that are strong enough to succeed without them.
