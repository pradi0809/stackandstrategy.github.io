2a:T27fd,<p>There is something interesting about managers who always seem to be in the middle of every problem.</p>
<p>A production issue occurs, and they are the first to jump in. A project gets delayed, and they immediately take over. A stakeholder raises a concern, and they personally start coordinating the resolution. A team member struggles with a decision, and they step in to make it.</p>
<p>They are always available, always involved, and seemingly always busy.</p>
<p>From the outside, these managers often look like dedicated leaders who go above and beyond for their teams.</p>
<p>But I think there is a question worth asking:</p>
<p><strong>Are they actually building a stronger organization, or are they building an organization that cannot function without them?</strong></p>
<p>I call this the hero syndrome in leadership.</p>
<p>It is the tendency of managers to position themselves as the person who can solve every problem, make every important decision, and rescue every difficult situation.</p>
<p>Initially, this behavior might even be rewarded. Stakeholders appreciate the quick response. Senior leadership sees someone taking ownership. Teams appreciate having someone who can step in when things get difficult.</p>
<p>But over time, the same behavior can become one of the biggest organizational bottlenecks.</p>
<h2 id="the-manager-who-always-saves-the-day">The manager who always saves the day</h2>
<p>Consider a familiar situation in a technology organization.</p>
<p>A critical delivery milestone is at risk. There are dependencies across engineering, infrastructure, security, and business teams.</p>
<p>The manager recognizes the urgency and immediately gets involved.</p>
<p>They start coordinating meetings, following up with individual contributors, negotiating timelines, making technical decisions, and communicating updates to senior stakeholders.</p>
<p>Eventually, the milestone is delivered.</p>
<p>Everyone appreciates the manager's contribution.</p>
<p>But something important has happened along the way.</p>
<p>The engineers have not learned how to independently manage cross-functional dependencies. The technical leads have not developed the confidence to drive difficult conversations. The next-level managers have not had the opportunity to demonstrate ownership.</p>
<p>The manager has solved the immediate problem, but the organization has not necessarily become better at solving similar problems.</p>
<p>The next time a comparable situation occurs, everyone looks to the same manager.</p>
<p>And the cycle repeats.</p>
<p>The manager becomes busier, the team becomes increasingly dependent, and the organization starts treating individual heroics as a normal operating model.</p>
<p><strong>When the same person has to repeatedly rescue the organization, perhaps the problem isn't the absence of a hero. Perhaps the problem is the operating model.</strong></p>
<h2 id="why-managers-fall-into-the-hero-trap">Why managers fall into the hero trap</h2>
<p>I don't necessarily believe managers consciously set out to create dependency.</p>
<p>In many cases, the behavior comes from good intentions.</p>
<p>They want things done correctly. They don't want their teams to fail. They feel accountable for outcomes and believe that stepping in is the fastest way to get things moving.</p>
<p>There are also organizational incentives that reinforce this behavior.</p>
<p>A manager who personally resolves a critical issue is visible. A manager who quietly develops three capable leaders might receive much less recognition.</p>
<p>Immediate results are easier to demonstrate than long-term capability building.</p>
<p>But there is a difference between taking ownership and taking over.</p>
<p>Taking ownership means ensuring the problem gets resolved, even when the actual work is performed by someone else.</p>
<p>Taking over means assuming responsibility for activities and decisions that should belong to the team.</p>
<p>The first is leadership. The second can become dependency.</p>
<h2 id="the-hidden-cost-of-being-indispensable">The hidden cost of being indispensable</h2>
<p>The hero syndrome has consequences that are not always immediately visible.</p>
<p><strong>1. It creates a decision-making bottleneck</strong></p>
<p>When every important decision needs managerial intervention, execution slows down.</p>
<p>People stop evaluating alternatives independently because they expect the manager to make the final call.</p>
<p>Even experienced architects and engineering managers may hesitate to move forward without approval.</p>
<p><strong>2. It limits the development of future leaders</strong></p>
<p>People develop leadership capability by being given opportunities to lead.</p>
<p>If the manager always takes over difficult conversations, stakeholder escalations, and critical decisions, the next generation of leaders never gets enough exposure.</p>
<p>They remain good executors but struggle to become independent leaders.</p>
<p><strong>3. It creates an unsustainable workload</strong></p>
<p>The manager becomes the escalation point for everything.</p>
<p>Routine decisions, operational issues, project dependencies, and stakeholder conversations all compete for the same person's attention.</p>
<p>Eventually, strategic thinking gets displaced by operational firefighting.</p>
<p><strong>4. It creates a false sense of organizational strength</strong></p>
<p>The organization may appear to be performing well because one individual is compensating for weaknesses across the system.</p>
<p>But what happens when that person is unavailable, changes roles, or leaves?</p>
<p>The dependency becomes visible very quickly.</p>
<p>An organization should not confuse the effectiveness of an individual hero with the maturity of its operating model.</p>
<h2 id="the-difference-between-stepping-in-and-taking-over">The difference between stepping in and taking over</h2>
<p>I don't believe leaders should stay away from problems just to demonstrate that they are delegating.</p>
<p>There are situations where direct intervention is necessary.</p>
<p>A major security incident, a business-critical outage, a serious compliance concern, or a decision with significant enterprise-wide consequences may require immediate leadership involvement.</p>
<p>The distinction is in what happens after the immediate situation is under control.</p>
<p>For example, consider a recurring vulnerability remediation problem.</p>
<p>A manager could personally coordinate every application owner, chase every remediation commitment, and escalate every delay.</p>
<p>That might improve short-term visibility.</p>
<p>But an enabling leader would look at the underlying system.</p>
<p>Are ownership responsibilities clear? Are remediation timelines defined? Are dependencies tracked? Do application teams have the resources to upgrade? Are recurring blockers being addressed? Is there sufficient automation?</p>
<p>The leader should establish accountability, remove organizational barriers, and ensure the right operating mechanisms exist.</p>
<p>The team should own execution.</p>
<p>The objective is not to make the manager less accountable. It is to make the organization less dependent on the manager's personal intervention.</p>
<h2 id="your-real-job-is-to-develop-people-who-can-replace-you">Your real job is to develop people who can replace you</h2>
<p>This is perhaps the most important shift in mindset.</p>
<p>As managers, we should actively create opportunities for others to take ownership of responsibilities that we previously handled ourselves.</p>
<p>Let a technical lead run the architecture discussion.</p>
<p>Let an engineering manager handle a difficult stakeholder conversation.</p>
<p>Let a senior engineer lead a production incident review.</p>
<p>Let someone else present the team's progress to senior leadership.</p>
<p>They may not handle everything exactly the way you would.</p>
<p>They may make mistakes. They may need guidance. They may take longer initially.</p>
<p>That is part of developing capability.</p>
<p>The manager's role is to provide context, set expectations, establish boundaries, coach, and intervene when necessary.</p>
<p>It is not to ensure that everyone performs tasks in exactly the same way the manager would.</p>
<p>If you never give people the opportunity to operate independently, you cannot expect them to become independent.</p>
<h2 id="my-perspective-stop-celebrating-organizational-dependency">My perspective: Stop celebrating organizational dependency</h2>
<p>I have come to believe that being indispensable is often an overrated measure of leadership success.</p>
<p>A manager who personally resolves every difficult situation may be a very capable individual contributor, but that alone does not make them an effective organizational leader.</p>
<p>Leadership is about multiplying capability.</p>
<p>It is about creating systems, developing people, establishing accountability, and building an environment where good decisions can happen at every level.</p>
<p>I would rather see an organization where five leaders can independently manage their respective areas than one manager who personally controls all five.</p>
<p>I would rather see a team that can resolve problems without waiting for managerial instructions than one that consistently depends on a manager to rescue it.</p>
<p>And I would rather spend time developing the next generation of leaders than constantly proving that I can solve every problem myself.</p>
<p>There is a simple test I would encourage every manager to consider:</p>
<p><strong>If your absence creates confusion, delays, and uncertainty across the organization, you may have built dependency instead of leadership capability.</strong></p>
<p>Your success should not be measured by how many problems require your intervention.</p>
<p>It should be measured by how many problems your team can solve because of the capability you helped them develop.</p>
<p>Don't aim to be the hero everyone depends on.</p>
<p>Aim to be the leader who makes heroes unnecessary.</p>
