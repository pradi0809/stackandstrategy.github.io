2f:T348c,<p>One of the most concerning things I have observed in organizations is not a lack of technical skills or experience.</p>
<p>It is the gradual disappearance of independent thinking.</p>
<p>You attend a meeting and ask for recommendations.</p>
<p>Nobody offers one.</p>
<p>You ask a technical lead how they would approach a problem.</p>
<p>They respond by asking what you would prefer.</p>
<p>You assign an initiative and expect the person responsible to come back with a plan.</p>
<p>Instead, they return with a list of questions about what they should do next.</p>
<p>At some point, you begin to wonder whether the team has the necessary capability to operate independently.</p>
<p>But I think there is another possibility worth considering.</p>
<p><strong>What if the organization has gradually trained people not to think for themselves?</strong></p>
<p>It doesn't happen overnight.</p>
<p>It develops through repeated interactions, management practices, and organizational behaviors that unintentionally discourage initiative.</p>
<h2 id="how-independent-thinking-disappears">How independent thinking disappears</h2>
<p>Consider a technical design discussion.</p>
<p>An architect presents a proposed solution.</p>
<p>The manager immediately questions the approach, suggests an alternative, and directs the architect to revise the design.</p>
<p>The architect goes back and makes the changes.</p>
<p>A few weeks later, another design is presented.</p>
<p>The same thing happens.</p>
<p>Eventually, the architect stops putting forward a recommendation.</p>
<p>Instead, they ask the manager what approach they should take before investing time in developing a solution.</p>
<p>From the manager's perspective, the architect may appear to lack initiative.</p>
<p>But from the architect's perspective, there is little incentive to exercise independent judgment when the manager consistently overrides it.</p>
<p>The behavior is learned.</p>
<p>People begin to understand what is expected of them.</p>
<p>They stop preparing recommendations and start preparing options for someone else to decide.</p>
<p>They stop challenging assumptions and start seeking agreement.</p>
<p>They stop owning outcomes and start following instructions.</p>
<p>This is how excessive control gradually weakens independent thinking.</p>
<h2 id="the-approval-culture">The approval culture</h2>
<p>Another common symptom is an organization where almost everything requires approval.</p>
<p>A technical lead cannot finalize a design without managerial sign-off.</p>
<p>An engineering manager cannot adjust priorities without seeking permission.</p>
<p>A team member cannot communicate a delivery risk to a stakeholder without first consulting their manager.</p>
<p>Even routine operational decisions become escalation points.</p>
<p>Of course, some decisions should require approval.</p>
<p>Financial commitments, significant architectural changes, security exceptions, and business-critical risks may need formal governance.</p>
<p>But when the approval process extends to routine decisions, it creates unnecessary dependency.</p>
<p>It also sends a message.</p>
<p>The message is that independent judgment is not trusted.</p>
<p>Over time, people adapt.</p>
<p>Why take responsibility for a decision when someone else can make it?</p>
<p>Why risk disagreement when approval is safer?</p>
<p>Why think through alternatives when the manager will eventually prescribe the answer?</p>
<p>The organization ends up with a culture of compliance rather than ownership.</p>
<h2 id="the-difference-between-asking-questions-and-challenging-thinking">The difference between asking questions and challenging thinking</h2>
<p>I believe managers play an important role in developing independent thinking.</p>
<p>But this requires changing how we interact with people.</p>
<p>Imagine an engineer approaching you with a technical problem.</p>
<p>One response is to immediately provide the solution.</p>
<p>You know the technology, understand the architecture, and have probably encountered something similar before.</p>
<p>Providing the answer is quick.</p>
<p>But consider a different response.</p>
<p>Ask the engineer:</p>
<ul>
<li>What do you believe is causing the problem?</li>
<li>What alternatives have you considered?</li>
<li>What are the trade-offs?</li>
<li>Which approach would you recommend?</li>
<li>What risks do you see?</li>
<li>What would you do if I weren't available?</li>
</ul>
<p>The conversation becomes an opportunity to develop reasoning.</p>
<p>You can challenge assumptions, introduce considerations they may have missed, and help them evaluate alternatives.</p>
<p>But the individual still owns the thinking.</p>
<p>I don't believe a manager needs to have the answer to every question.</p>
<p>Sometimes, asking the right question is far more valuable than providing an immediate solution.</p>
<p>This is particularly important in technology leadership, where problems are rarely identical and solutions require context-specific judgment.</p>
<p>We should be developing people who can reason through unfamiliar situations, not simply reproduce solutions from previous experiences.</p>
<h2 id="psychological-safety-and-the-fear-of-being-wrong">Psychological safety and the fear of being wrong</h2>
<p>There is another important dimension to this problem.</p>
<p>People will not exercise independent judgment if they believe every mistake will be treated as a failure.</p>
<p>Think about an engineering team working on a modernization initiative.</p>
<p>One engineer proposes an alternative architecture.</p>
<p>The proposal is challenged aggressively in a meeting. The engineer is criticized for not considering every possible dependency.</p>
<p>The discussion ends with the manager prescribing the solution.</p>
<p>What does the rest of the team learn?</p>
<p>Probably not that they should bring better proposals next time.</p>
<p>They learn that proposing something different can be risky.</p>
<p>They begin to keep their ideas to themselves.</p>
<p>This is why I believe psychological safety matters in leadership.</p>
<p>It doesn't mean lowering standards or avoiding difficult conversations.</p>
<p>It means creating an environment where people can disagree respectfully, raise concerns, challenge decisions, and admit uncertainty without fear of humiliation.</p>
<p>A team should be able to say:</p>
<p>"I disagree with this approach, and here is why."</p>
<p>It should also be able to say:</p>
<p>"I made the wrong decision. Here is what happened, and here is what I learned."</p>
<p>Leaders need to distinguish between an honest mistake made through responsible decision-making and careless behavior that disregards established expectations.</p>
<p>If we want people to think independently, we must make it safe for them to express independent thinking.</p>
<h2 id="when-agreement-becomes-more-important-than-good-decisions">When agreement becomes more important than good decisions</h2>
<p>I have always believed that a team where everyone agrees with the manager is not necessarily a healthy team.</p>
<p>In fact, it can be a warning sign.</p>
<p>Consider an architecture review where everyone accepts the proposed solution without questioning its scalability, security implications, operational complexity, or long-term cost.</p>
<p>The meeting finishes quickly.</p>
<p>But has the organization made a good decision?</p>
<p>Perhaps not.</p>
<p>Healthy technical organizations need constructive disagreement.</p>
<p>Architects should challenge engineering decisions. Engineers should question architectural assumptions. Security teams should identify risks. Operations teams should challenge designs that create unnecessary operational complexity.</p>
<p>The objective isn't to win arguments.</p>
<p>It is to make better decisions.</p>
<p>A leader should welcome a well-reasoned disagreement, even when it challenges their own recommendation.</p>
<p>If people learn that agreement is rewarded more consistently than critical thinking, they will eventually optimize for agreement.</p>
<p>And the organization will lose one of its most valuable capabilities: the ability to challenge itself.</p>
<h2 id="autonomy-needs-guardrails-not-constant-supervision">Autonomy needs guardrails, not constant supervision</h2>
<p>There is an important distinction between giving people autonomy and allowing everyone to operate without alignment.</p>
<p>Independent thinking does not mean independent direction.</p>
<p>Teams need shared objectives, architectural principles, security standards, financial boundaries, and clear accountability.</p>
<p>For example, an engineering organization can establish enterprise architecture standards and security requirements while allowing individual teams to determine how best to implement solutions within those boundaries.</p>
<p>Similarly, a platform engineering organization can provide golden paths, standardized deployment pipelines, and reusable capabilities without dictating every engineering decision.</p>
<p>The purpose of guardrails is to make responsible decision-making easier.</p>
<p>They should not become another mechanism for centralizing control.</p>
<p>Good leadership creates clarity about what cannot be compromised while allowing flexibility in how outcomes are achieved.</p>
<h2 id="rebuilding-a-culture-of-independent-thinking">Rebuilding a culture of independent thinking</h2>
<p>If a team has become accustomed to seeking instructions, changing that behavior requires patience and consistency.</p>
<p>Managers cannot spend years centralizing decisions and suddenly expect everyone to become autonomous.</p>
<p>It requires deliberate effort.</p>
<p>A few practices can make a meaningful difference.</p>
<p><strong>Ask for recommendations, not just updates.</strong></p>
<p>Instead of asking what someone has completed, ask what they recommend doing next and why.</p>
<p><strong>Give people ownership of decisions.</strong></p>
<p>Start with decisions that have manageable risks and gradually expand their authority.</p>
<p><strong>Encourage constructive disagreement.</strong></p>
<p>Make it clear that challenging an idea is not the same as challenging someone's authority.</p>
<p><strong>Avoid immediately providing answers.</strong></p>
<p>Give people time to think, evaluate, and propose solutions before stepping in.</p>
<p><strong>Recognize good judgment, not just successful outcomes.</strong></p>
<p>A well-reasoned decision can still produce an unexpected result. Recognize the quality of the thinking, not only whether the outcome was perfect.</p>
<p><strong>Be consistent.</strong></p>
<p>If managers encourage independent thinking but repeatedly override decisions without explanation, the culture will not change.</p>
<p>The behavior of leadership must match the expectations being placed on the team.</p>
<h2 id="my-perspective-dont-create-followers-when-you-need-thinkers">My perspective: Don't create followers when you need thinkers</h2>
<p>I believe one of the biggest responsibilities of leadership is to develop people who can think beyond the instructions they receive.</p>
<p>A team that executes instructions efficiently has value.</p>
<p>But a team that can identify problems, challenge assumptions, evaluate alternatives, make decisions, and take ownership creates much greater organizational capability.</p>
<p>This becomes even more important as technology organizations evolve.</p>
<p>With automation and AI increasingly taking over routine execution, the value of human judgment, problem-solving, critical thinking, and decision-making becomes even more important.</p>
<p>We should be building teams that can think, not simply teams that can execute.</p>
<p>I would rather have an engineer who challenges my technical recommendation with sound reasoning than one who agrees with everything I say.</p>
<p>I would rather have an architect who identifies a weakness in an enterprise design than one who simply implements it without question.</p>
<p>And I would rather have an engineering manager who independently resolves difficult situations than one who constantly escalates decisions that should be theirs to make.</p>
<p>As leaders, we should periodically ask ourselves:</p>
<p><strong>Are we developing people who can make better decisions, or are we developing people who are simply getting better at asking us for decisions?</strong></p>
<p>There is a significant difference.</p>
<p>If your team has stopped thinking independently, don't start by demanding more initiative.</p>
<p>First, examine whether your leadership practices actually encourage it.</p>
<p>Because people don't develop ownership simply because it appears in their job description.</p>
<p>They develop it when they are trusted, given opportunities, supported through challenges, and held accountable for meaningful outcomes.</p>
<p><strong>The goal of leadership is not to create a team that follows instructions perfectly. It is to create a team that understands the purpose, thinks critically, makes sound decisions, and takes responsibility for the results.</strong></p>
<p>Don't measure your team's effectiveness by how well they follow your instructions.</p>
<p>Measure it by how well they think and perform when you aren't giving any.</p>
