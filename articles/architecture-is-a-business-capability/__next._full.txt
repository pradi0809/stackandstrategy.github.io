17:T2652,<p>Some time ago, I watched an organization spend the better part of six months producing a target-state architecture for a modernization program.</p>
<p>The document was impressive. Sixty-odd pages. Layered capability maps, integration views, a data architecture, a transition roadmap, and a beautifully colour-coded picture of how everything would look in three years.</p>
<p>It was presented to the steering committee, praised, approved, and filed.</p>
<p>Eighteen months later, I asked a delivery lead on one of the programs how the target state was influencing their work.</p>
<p>They paused, then asked, <strong>"Which version?"</strong></p>
<p>That answer has stayed with me. Not because the architects did poor work. They were capable people who did exactly what they had been asked to do. But what they had been asked to produce was the wrong thing.</p>
<p>To me, architecture is not the production of diagrams, standards, and review meetings. <strong>The real purpose of architecture is to improve the quality, speed, and durability of decisions.</strong> Everything else is a by-product.</p>
<h2 id="start-with-the-decision-not-the-drawing">Start with the decision, not the drawing</h2>
<p>Consider two architects given the same problem.</p>
<p>A business unit wants to launch a new customer onboarding journey. Customer data is spread across a CRM platform, a legacy policy system, and a handful of spreadsheets that nobody likes to talk about.</p>
<p>The first architect starts drawing. Current state, target state, integration patterns, a canonical data model. Three weeks later there is a comprehensive pack. It is accurate. It is also too broad for anyone to act on, and the delivery team has already started building because the business deadline did not wait.</p>
<p>The second architect starts with a question: <strong>what decision do we actually need to make, and by when?</strong></p>
<p>It turns out there are only three that matter in the next quarter:</p>
<ul>
<li>Where should the customer master record live?</li>
<li>Do we integrate directly with the legacy system or put an API layer in front of it?</li>
<li>What is the minimum data quality we need before launch?</li>
</ul>
<p>The architect writes a short decision record for each one. The options, the trade-offs, the recommendation, and the risks being accepted. Each is two pages at most. The diagrams that exist are there because they help explain a choice, not because a template required them.</p>
<p>Both architects worked hard. Only one of them changed the outcome.</p>
<p>I'd rather have a one-page decision record that people actually use than forty diagrams that people admire.</p>
<h2 id="architecture-that-cannot-be-operated-is-not-architecture">Architecture that cannot be operated is not architecture</h2>
<p>One of the most expensive mistakes I've seen is a technically elegant design that the organization simply could not run.</p>
<p>A team, enthusiastic about event-driven architecture, introduced a streaming platform to decouple a set of order-processing services. On paper, it was the right pattern. Loose coupling, independent scaling, a clean audit trail.</p>
<p>In practice, nobody in the operations team had run that platform before. Monitoring was an afterthought. Consumer lag went unnoticed until customers started calling. When a partition issue surfaced on a Saturday night, the only person who understood the setup was on holiday.</p>
<p>Within a year, the organization was paying for a specialist support contract and quietly routing the most important flows back through the old synchronous integration "for stability."</p>
<p>The pattern was not wrong. The architecture was.</p>
<p>Good architecture takes the operating reality into account from the start: the skills the teams actually have, the support model, security controls, funding cycles, and how much change the organization can absorb. <strong>A design that ignores those constraints isn't ambitious. It's incomplete.</strong></p>
<p>I believe an architect should be able to answer a simple question about any significant recommendation: <em>who will run this at 2 a.m., and do they know how?</em></p>
<h2 id="governance-should-create-guardrails-not-queues">Governance should create guardrails, not queues</h2>
<p>Most organizations eventually create an architecture review board. The intent is good: consistency, risk management, alignment with enterprise principles.</p>
<p>But I've seen too many boards turn into queues.</p>
<p>Teams prepare elaborate submissions. They wait two or three weeks for a slot. They receive a list of questions, revise, and return. The well-run teams experience it as a tax. The teams that most need guidance learn to present their designs in a way that passes the review, then build something slightly different.</p>
<p>When that happens, the board isn't governing architecture. It is governing paperwork.</p>
<p>The approach I find far more effective has three parts:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A small number of non-negotiable rules.</strong> Security, data residency, identity, and a handful of integration standards. These are enforced, ideally through automation rather than meetings.</li>
<li><strong>Paved roads.</strong> Reference architectures, templates, and platform defaults that make the right way the easiest way.</li>
<li><strong>Decision records owned by the teams.</strong> Visible to everyone, reviewed by architects when the risk or cost justifies it, not by default.</li>
</ul>
<p>Within those boundaries, teams decide. Architects spend their time where the stakes are high, not reviewing the fifteenth variation of a standard web application.</p>
<p>And if teams consistently route around the architecture function, I don't see that as a compliance problem. <strong>I see it as feedback.</strong> It usually means the function is offering less value than it is costing in time.</p>
<h2 id="optimize-for-change-not-for-the-picture">Optimize for change, not for the picture</h2>
<p>The best architecture is rarely the one with the most components or the newest technology. It is the one that lets the organization respond to change without repeatedly rebuilding its foundations.</p>
<p>A simple example illustrates this.</p>
<p>I came across an environment where fourteen applications read from and wrote to the same shared database. It had started as a sensible shortcut years earlier. By the time anyone looked closely, a single column change required impact analysis across six teams, a coordinated release, and roughly three months of calendar time.</p>
<p>Nobody had designed it to be that way. It had simply accumulated, one reasonable decision at a time.</p>
<p>No target-state diagram would have fixed that. What fixed it, gradually, was a series of deliberate decisions: put an API in front of the most contested data, give each domain clear ownership of its records, and stop new applications from connecting directly.</p>
<p>That is what optimizing for change looks like in practice. <strong>Deliberate boundaries, clear ownership, observable systems, and an honest understanding of coupling.</strong> It is rarely glamorous, and it rarely shows up well in a slide. But it is the difference between a technology estate that can evolve and one that has to be replaced.</p>
<h2 id="measure-architecture-by-what-it-improves">Measure architecture by what it improves</h2>
<p>If architecture is a business capability, it should be measured like one.</p>
<p>Not by the number of documents produced or reviews completed, but by the decisions it improves and the friction it removes. The measures I find most useful are:</p>
<ul>
<li>How long does it take to make a significant technology decision, from question to commitment?</li>
<li>Is lead time for change getting shorter in the areas where architecture has been involved?</li>
<li>Are the same integration and data problems recurring, or are they being designed out?</li>
<li>Is operational risk reducing, and is ownership becoming clearer?</li>
</ul>
<p>These are harder to report than artifact counts. They also tell you whether the function is actually working.</p>
<h2 id="my-perspective-architects-should-own-outcomes">My perspective: architects should own outcomes</h2>
<p>I have come to believe that the most important change an architecture function can make is to stay with its decisions after they are made.</p>
<p>An architect who recommends an approach and then moves on before it reaches production has given advice. Valuable, perhaps. But architecture, as I see it, includes being accountable for whether the decision held up. Did the integration stabilize? Did the platform land within the operating cost we expected? Did the team become faster, or did we simply move complexity somewhere less visible?</p>
<p>When architects carry that accountability, their recommendations change. They become more pragmatic. "It depends" starts arriving with an explanation of what it depends <em>on</em>. The relationship with delivery teams shifts from reviewer and reviewed to people solving the same problem.</p>
<p>There are environments, particularly regulated ones, where artifacts genuinely matter. Auditors need evidence, and control mappings need to exist. I don't dismiss that. But I'd be careful not to let the audit format become the design process for everything else.</p>
<p><strong>My view is simple: architecture is a business capability, not a drawing practice. Its job is to help the organization make better technology decisions, make them faster, and make them executable. If it isn't doing that, more diagrams won't help.</strong></p>
