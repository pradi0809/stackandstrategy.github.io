23:T2b30,<p>Delegation is one of the most frequently discussed management skills.</p>
<p>Almost every leadership conversation emphasizes the importance of delegating work, empowering teams, and developing people.</p>
<p>Yet, in practice, I have seen a significant difference between what managers believe delegation means and what effective delegation actually looks like.</p>
<p>Some managers delegate by assigning tasks.</p>
<p>They tell someone what needs to be done, provide a deadline, and expect the work to be completed.</p>
<p>Others delegate by transferring ownership.</p>
<p>They explain the desired outcome, provide the necessary context, clarify decision-making authority, establish expectations, and allow the individual to determine how to deliver.</p>
<p>These two approaches might look similar initially.</p>
<p>But their long-term impact on people and organizations is very different.</p>
<p><strong>Assigning work creates activity. Transferring ownership creates accountability.</strong></p>
<h2 id="the-familiar-delegation-problem">The familiar delegation problem</h2>
<p>Imagine a manager asking an engineering lead to take ownership of a platform modernization initiative.</p>
<p>The manager says:</p>
<p>"We need to upgrade the platform. Put together a plan, coordinate with the teams, track the progress, and make sure we finish by the end of the quarter."</p>
<p>The engineering lead accepts the responsibility.</p>
<p>A few days later, they discover that several application teams have competing priorities. Infrastructure resources are limited. Security requirements need clarification. There are architectural dependencies that require decisions from another group.</p>
<p>The lead starts coordinating meetings and following up with stakeholders.</p>
<p>But whenever a decision needs to be made, they return to the manager.</p>
<p>The manager responds by making the decisions, negotiating with other teams, and providing further instructions.</p>
<p>Eventually, the engineering lead becomes responsible for tracking and coordinating the initiative, while the manager retains actual ownership of the important decisions.</p>
<p>This is a common delegation failure.</p>
<p>The responsibility has been assigned, but the authority has not been transferred.</p>
<p>The individual is accountable for an outcome they cannot fully control.</p>
<p>That is not empowerment.</p>
<h2 id="delegation-requires-more-than-assigning-a-task">Delegation requires more than assigning a task</h2>
<p>I believe effective delegation has five essential elements.</p>
<h3 id="1-clarity-of-outcomes">1. Clarity of outcomes</h3>
<p>People need to understand what success looks like.</p>
<p>Instead of saying, "Improve our deployment process," explain the intended outcome.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<p>"We want engineering teams to independently provision environments and deploy applications through standardized pipelines, reducing manual dependencies and improving deployment consistency."</p>
<p>The second statement provides context and direction.</p>
<p>It allows the person to evaluate different approaches instead of simply following instructions.</p>
<h3 id="2-authority-to-make-decisions">2. Authority to make decisions</h3>
<p>Responsibility without decision-making authority creates frustration.</p>
<p>If someone owns a modernization initiative, they should have the authority to make appropriate technical and operational decisions within agreed boundaries.</p>
<p>This doesn't mean every decision should be unrestricted.</p>
<p>Architecture standards, security requirements, financial approvals, and enterprise policies still matter.</p>
<p>But the boundaries should be explicit.</p>
<p>People should know which decisions they can make independently, which require consultation, and which need formal approval.</p>
<h3 id="3-access-to-resources">3. Access to resources</h3>
<p>Delegation also requires enabling people with the resources needed to succeed.</p>
<p>That includes engineering capacity, tools, information, stakeholder access, and organizational support.</p>
<p>Assigning someone a critical initiative without addressing resource constraints is not delegation.</p>
<p>It is setting them up for failure.</p>
<h3 id="4-accountability-and-measurable-expectations">4. Accountability and measurable expectations</h3>
<p>Ownership should be accompanied by measurable outcomes.</p>
<p>For a platform engineering initiative, these might include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Number of applications onboarded to standardized deployment patterns.</li>
<li>Reduction in manual deployment activities.</li>
<li>Adoption of self-service capabilities.</li>
<li>Improvement in deployment frequency and reliability.</li>
<li>Reduction in operational dependencies.</li>
</ul>
<p>The objective is to evaluate results, not continuously monitor every activity.</p>
<h3 id="5-support-without-interference">5. Support without interference</h3>
<p>This is where leadership judgment becomes important.</p>
<p>The manager should remain available to coach, challenge, and remove obstacles.</p>
<p>But support should not become constant interference.</p>
<p>There is a difference between asking someone how they are progressing and telling them exactly how to execute every step.</p>
<p>The former creates accountability.</p>
<p>The latter can undermine ownership.</p>
<h2 id="the-difference-between-a-status-update-and-an-ownership-conversation">The difference between a status update and an ownership conversation</h2>
<p>I also think managers should reconsider the way they conduct follow-ups.</p>
<p>A typical status conversation often revolves around questions such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>What did you complete yesterday?</li>
<li>What are you working on today?</li>
<li>Why is this taking longer?</li>
<li>Have you followed up with everyone?</li>
<li>When will this be completed?</li>
</ul>
<p>These questions are not necessarily wrong. Operational visibility matters.</p>
<p>But if every conversation focuses on individual activities, managers can unintentionally create a culture where people optimize for reporting work rather than owning outcomes.</p>
<p>An ownership-oriented conversation is different.</p>
<p>It asks:</p>
<ul>
<li>Are we on track to achieve the intended outcome?</li>
<li>What risks could prevent us from succeeding?</li>
<li>What decisions do you need to make?</li>
<li>Which dependencies are outside your control?</li>
<li>What support do you need from me?</li>
<li>What would you change if you were responsible for this initiative end to end?</li>
</ul>
<p>The difference is subtle but meaningful.</p>
<p>The manager is still engaged, but the individual is expected to think, evaluate, and lead.</p>
<h2 id="delegation-also-means-allowing-people-to-make-mistakes">Delegation also means allowing people to make mistakes</h2>
<p>This is perhaps the hardest part for many managers.</p>
<p>We often delegate work but struggle to accept that someone might approach it differently from how we would.</p>
<p>When an individual makes a decision that doesn't produce the expected outcome, the immediate reaction can be to step in, take control, and prevent further mistakes.</p>
<p>Sometimes that intervention is necessary.</p>
<p>But if every mistake results in the manager taking back ownership, people quickly learn not to make independent decisions.</p>
<p>They become cautious.</p>
<p>They seek approval for everything.</p>
<p>They avoid responsibility because the cost of getting something wrong is too high.</p>
<p>I believe managers need to distinguish between mistakes caused by negligence and mistakes that happen as part of learning and exercising judgment.</p>
<p>A technical decision that doesn't work as expected can become a valuable learning opportunity.</p>
<p>The important questions are whether the decision was made responsibly, whether the risks were understood, whether appropriate guardrails existed, and whether the individual learned from the outcome.</p>
<p>If we expect people to develop judgment, we must give them opportunities to exercise it.</p>
<h2 id="delegation-changes-as-people-grow">Delegation changes as people grow</h2>
<p>Not everyone needs the same level of delegation.</p>
<p>A new engineer might need detailed guidance, frequent feedback, and clearly defined tasks.</p>
<p>An experienced technical lead should be able to own a technical workstream and coordinate dependencies independently.</p>
<p>An engineering manager should be capable of managing delivery, people, and stakeholder expectations.</p>
<p>A senior leader should be able to translate business objectives into organizational priorities and develop the next level of leadership.</p>
<p>The degree of direction should evolve with the individual's experience, capability, and the complexity of the work.</p>
<p>Treating an experienced leader like a junior team member is counterproductive.</p>
<p>Equally, giving someone a broad responsibility without the necessary preparation or support is not empowerment.</p>
<p>Good delegation requires judgment.</p>
<p>The manager needs to understand the individual's strengths, identify development opportunities, and gradually expand their decision-making responsibilities.</p>
<h2 id="my-perspective-delegate-ownership-not-just-workload">My perspective: Delegate ownership, not just workload</h2>
<p>I don't see delegation as a way for managers to reduce their own workload.</p>
<p>That may be a benefit, but it shouldn't be the primary objective.</p>
<p>The real value of delegation is organizational capability development.</p>
<p>When I delegate something, I should be thinking about more than who can complete the task.</p>
<p>I should be thinking about who can grow by owning it.</p>
<p>Can this person lead a cross-functional discussion? Can they make a difficult technical decision? Can they manage stakeholder expectations? Can they develop a solution rather than wait for instructions?</p>
<p>These are opportunities to develop future leaders.</p>
<p>I also believe managers should be comfortable with the fact that delegated work may not be completed exactly the way they would have done it.</p>
<p>If the outcome meets expectations, the decisions are sound, and the approach respects organizational guardrails, different approaches should be welcomed.</p>
<p>The objective isn't to create a team of people who think exactly like the manager.</p>
<p>It is to create a team of people who can think independently and deliver collectively.</p>
<p>Ultimately, delegation should follow a simple principle:</p>
<p><strong>Give people the responsibility, authority, context, and resources to own an outcome. Stay available to support them, but resist the temptation to take control back at the first sign of difficulty.</strong></p>
<p>If you delegate tasks, you get people who execute.</p>
<p>If you delegate ownership, you develop people who lead.</p>
